I have sat through a lot of planning sessions. Goals laid out on a slide, milestones shaded by quarter, an owner assigned to every box, the whole thing looking like a finished piece of analysis. And then the year ends and almost none of it happened, and nobody is entirely sure why.

The reflex is to blame execution. Sometimes that is the truth. But more often, looking back, the problem was that we never actually made a strategic decision at all. We made a list.

Michael Porter's 1996 paper [What Is Strategy?](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy) is blunt about this: the essence of strategy is choosing a unique and valuable position, and the reason a position is valuable is precisely because it involves trade-offs. If you have not excluded something, you have not chosen. A plan that fits every plausible priority has selected nothing.

Roger Martin puts the same point more plainly in a recent [Harvard Business Review masterclass](https://hbr.org/2025/05/a-plan-is-not-a-strategy): strategy is a set of choices that fit together and reinforce one another. A choice only counts if it is exclusive. "We will do all of the above" is not a choice; it is a postponement.

So how do you actually get better at this? Not by reading more strategy books, though those help. You get better at it by changing the questions you ask, and by having somewhere to put the answers.

## Most strategy documents are not strategy

Richard Rumelt's [Good Strategy, Bad Strategy](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy) is the most useful corrective I have read, because it defines the failure mode precisely. Good strategy has three parts: a **diagnosis** (a clear statement of the nature of the challenge), a **guiding policy** (the overall approach for dealing with it), and **coherent action** (the coordinated set of things that carry the policy out).

Against that, Rumelt names four hallmarks of bad strategy:

- **Fluff** — a long list of aspirational words standing in for a thought
- **Failure to face the challenge** — pretending there is no crux, or that effort alone will dissolve it
- **Goals instead of strategy** — stating a desired destination and calling the ambition a plan
- **Bad objectives** — targets that are incoherent with each other or impossible with the resources available

The reason this is so useful is that it is testable. Take any plan, any deck, any quarterly priorities document, and check it against three questions: is there a diagnosis that says something non-obvious about the problem? Is there an approach that rules something out? Do the actions reinforce each other rather than quietly competing for the same people and budget? If the answers are no, no, and mostly no, you are looking at a plan with a strategy-shaped hole in the middle.

Rumelt is equally scathing about using the framework as a template. Three headings filled with three paragraphs of nothing is still nothing.

## The skills that make up strategic thinking

"Strategic thinking" sounds like a personality trait — some people have it, most do not. It is closer to a set of separate, learnable moves. Here are the ones that have done the most work for me.

### Diagnosis before prescription

The most common sequencing error is jumping to solutions. You want to improve retention, so you commission a loyalty programme. You want the team to ship faster, so you run a scrum training. Diagnosis is the discipline of writing one sentence that says what is actually going on, in language that would be recognisable to an informed insider — and that an informed insider might therefore disagree with.

A diagnosis is a judgement, not a summary. "Retention is low" is a symptom. "New users hit the paywall at minute four and roughly a third of them never reach a meaningful session, so our onboarding spends its effort on the wrong half of the cohort" is a diagnosis. It points at a specific lever, and it can be wrong in a testable way.

### The outside view and base rates

This is the highest-leverage idea I have come across and it costs nothing but humility. Michael Mauboussin's [work on the outside view](https://fs.blog/inside-view-michael-mauboussin/) is essentially: when you forecast, start from the reference class, not from the story of your specific case. What happens, on average, to projects like this one? What did the last twenty look like? Then adjust from there for whatever is genuinely different about yours.

Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner's [Superforecasting](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/224065/superforecasting-by-philip-e-tetlock-and-dan-gardner/) showed that trained, disciplined forecasters using base rates and continual updating can beat people with classified information and institutional authority. Per [Wikipedia's summary of the Good Judgment Project results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superforecaster), the top forecasters performed roughly 30% better than the average for intelligence community analysts who could read intercepts and other secret data. The [Good Judgment team reports](https://goodjudgment.com/resources/the-superforecasters-track-record/) that their tournament team beat the control group by more than 50%.

The uncomfortable implication is that the story you are telling yourself about why this will work is much weaker evidence than it feels. Not because your story is bad, but because narratives are what confident people produce when they have not checked.

The same logic applies to price and effort in his [expectations investing](https://www.expectationsinvesting.com/) work: start from what is already priced in, then ask what has to happen for the situation to justify itself. "What would have to be true for this to be a good idea?" is often more productive than "is this a good idea?"

### Inversion

Charlie Munger's version: [invert, always invert](https://fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment/). Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid that. It is more generative than it sounds, because failure causes are more concrete and more observable than success causes. Everyone knows what makes a launch go badly. Very few people can name the specific conditions for a launch to go well.

### Second-order thinking

And then what? The first-order effects of a decision are the ones that appear in the business case. The second-order effects are what happen after the people involved adjust their behaviour in response, which is where most of the damage lives. Discounts attract customers who were always going to buy. A hiring freeze feels like a cost saving right up until the point where the people doing the most valuable work are the ones who leave.

A good test: after you state the outcome you want, ask what it will cause, and then what that will cause. Keep going until you hit something you would not sign off on.

### Premortem

Gary Klein's [premortem](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem) inverts the timeline. Instead of "what could go wrong?", you say: it is a year from now, this failed completely, and write down why. His point is social as much as cognitive — in a normal planning meeting, the knowledgeable person who thinks the plan is weak has no socially acceptable way to say so. In a premortem, they are just reporting the outcome that everyone already half-suspects. Klein's method draws on "prospective hindsight", the effect reported in Mitchell, Russo and Pennington's 1989 work, where imagining that an outcome has already occurred made people substantially better at generating the reasons it might have.

Fifteen minutes, a whiteboard, a rule that nobody edits anybody else's reason. It reliably surfaces the objections that would have died in the room.

### Decision quality versus outcome quality

Annie Duke was a World Series of Poker champion before she was a decision researcher, and [Thinking in Bets](https://www.annieduke.com/thinking-in-bets/) is her attempt to make one domain explain the other. The core error she names is *resulting* — treating a good outcome as proof of a good decision, and a bad outcome as proof of a bad one, when the difference between them is often just luck.

The practical discipline is to write down the reasoning and the odds *before* you find out, then grade the thinking rather than the scoreboard. "We thought we had a 40% chance and the numbers looked like a 40% chance, so the process was sound and the result was just a coin landing" is a completely different kind of failure from "we had a 40% chance, told everyone it was a certainty, and were surprised". The first one teaches you nothing and costs you nothing. The second one teaches you something expensive.

### Tempo

John Boyd's [OODA loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop) — observe, orient, decide, act — was developed out of fighter combat, where the side that cycles faster and forces its opponent to respond to a reality that has already changed wins regardless of who started with better information. Applied to anything slower, the lesson is still the same: in a fast-moving environment, the quality of your loop matters more than the completeness of any single analysis. A slightly less thorough view that you act on, and then update, beats a perfect one that arrives too late to use.

## The questions to ask

This is the part I keep coming back to. Most strategic failures trace back to a question that was never asked, or asked too late. Here is the set I work through, grouped by what they are for.

**On the challenge itself**

- What is the hardest problem here, stated in one sentence, such that someone who disagreed with me would recognise the disagreement?
- What are we actually solving, versus what are we being asked to deliver?
- Which internal obstacle is the real one — inertia, politics, a protected project?
- What would have to be true for this to succeed, and which of those conditions is least within our control?

**On the choices**

- Where are we going to play, and where are we explicitly not?
- How will we win there, in terms a customer would recognise?
- What does this rule out? If nothing, it is not a choice.
- If a well-resourced competitor read this strategy, what is the cheapest counter they could run?
- Do these actions reinforce each other, or are three of them competing for the same two people?

**On the evidence**

- What is the base rate here? What happened, on average, in comparable situations?
- What am I assuming that I have not actually verified?
- Who disagrees with me, and what is the strongest version of their argument?
- What are the incentives of the people giving me this information?
- What evidence would change my mind? If I cannot answer that, I have a belief, not a strategy.

**On second and third order effects**

- And then what?
- What does this teach the people on the other side to do next time?
- What does this make easier for a competitor, a rival department, or a future version of me?
- What is the worst plausible second-order effect, and would I still choose this if I knew it was going to happen?

**On failure**

- It is a year later and this failed. What happened? Write it as fact, not as a risk.
- Which single assumption, if wrong, sinks everything?
- What are we avoiding, and is the avoidance rational?
- If this is the wrong call, how will we know early enough to reverse cheaply?

**On the bet and the review**

- What are our odds of this being right? What is the base rate for bets like this?
- What does the decision cost if we are wrong, and is that affordable?
- What decision-quality information would tell us to double down or cut losses, before the results come in?
- When will we review this, and what will have to be true by then?

Charlie Munger's advice to build a [latticework of mental models](https://fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment/) across disciplines is the underlying idea here. You are not trying to become a strategist. You are stacking a few different lenses — outside view, inversion, second-order effects, premortem, base rates — so that no single one of your habits can quietly run the whole show.

## Improving the quality of your thinking

The question bank is a tool, and a tool only performs as well as the judgement operating it. You can ask every question on that list brilliantly and still get it wrong, because the failure will have been in how you reasoned rather than in what you were asked. Improving the quality of thinking is a separate project from knowing what to think about, and it is the half of the discipline that is most often skipped.

Overconfidence is the most robust and widely documented cognitive bias there is, and the most dangerous property of it is that it feels identical from the inside whether you are right or wrong. [Metacognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacognition) — thinking about your own thinking — is the only mechanism I know of that reliably catches it, and it is the thing that decays fastest under pressure, volume and fatigue. A few things have measurably improved my own.

### Be a scout, not a soldier

Julia Galef's framing in [The Scout Mindset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scout_Mindset) is the cleanest one I have found: a *soldier* thinks to defend a position, a *scout* thinks to see things as they are. The soldier optimises for being right, the scout optimises for being accurate, and once the second one is your actual objective, changing your mind stops feeling like defeat.

This is not a temperament exercise, it is a procedural one, because the interesting part is the *when*. People are perfectly willing to argue both sides on a topic they do not care about, and completely incapable of doing it on a topic where their competence, reputation or paycheque is involved. So the discipline is to fix the moment in advance: decide the point at which you would change your position, and write it down before the argument starts. If you cannot name the point, or if the honest answer is that nothing would change your mind, you are a soldier with a view, and the risk is not that you are wrong but that you will never know.

The associated habit is cheap: for any important claim, write the strongest version of the opposing argument — not a caricature of it. The test of whether you have managed this is whether someone who holds that view would recognise it as a fair statement of their case.

### Test your understanding, do not admire it

The [illusion of explanatory depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth), demonstrated by Rozenblit and Keil, is the gap between being able to *predict* something and being able to *explain* it. The finding is uncomfortable: people consistently believe they understand causal mechanisms in far more detail than they do, and the belief is strongest exactly where the understanding is thinnest.

The corrective is to explain the thing out loud, mechanism by mechanism, until you reach a step where you discover you do not actually know what happens there. Do this for your own diagnosis, your own business case, and your own understanding of why a decision failed. It is unpleasant and it takes ten minutes, and it is the cheapest way I know to find the load-bearing assumption you have been treating as background knowledge. If you cannot complete the chain, you have a prediction, not a model — and you should say so out loud, which in most cases means going back to the base rate.

### Calibrate with numbers, not adjectives

"Confident" and "not confident" carry almost no information. A number does. The whole point of writing a probability rather than a prediction is that it forces you to commit and makes you gradeable later, and a decade of graded forecasts is the only reliable cure for miscalibration that I have found.

Superforecasters are distinguished less by being clever than by being well-calibrated and willing to revise — the [Tetlock research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_E._Tetlock) found that the people who update their beliefs most are the ones whose beliefs are worth updating. That is an uncomfortable pairing: the most confident-seeming people in most rooms are frequently the least accurate, and being wrong in a low-information environment never feels like being wrong at the time.

### Check whether your intuition has been earned

Kahneman and Klein's paper [Conditions for Intuitive Expertise](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755) is titled "A Failure to Disagree" and the title is the argument. The two sides of the intuition debate were both wrong, because they disagreed about whether intuition can be trusted, when the real answer is conditional. Expert intuition requires two things: a high-validity environment, where outcomes are regular and feedback is genuinely diagnostic, and enough opportunity to have learned those regularities.

Most of the situations people have strong opinions about are low-validity: markets, long-horizon bets, hiring, which platform will win. There, feedback is noisy, sparse or misleading, and the pattern you have "learned" is mostly noise. Gut feel in those settings is not weak evidence — it is evidence from an environment that cannot produce any. This is genuinely useful to know, because the correct response is not to suppress the intuition but to recognise that it is not informing you, and to substitute a different procedure entirely.

The related warning is the [Dunning–Kruger effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger_effect), which is domain-specific in a way that is underappreciated. Experience in one field does not transfer as confidence in the next; you re-enter every new domain on the left of the curve. And the [overconfidence effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overconfidence_effect) is not stupidity — experienced people are often better-calibrated on easy questions and only show the effect on hard ones, where self-assessment stops being a reliable proxy for accuracy.

### Know where your model breaks

The statistician George Box's line — [all models are wrong, some are useful](https://fs.blog/all-models-are-wrong/) — is not a disclaimer, it is a question. Which model am I using, what was it built to describe, and what is it doing here? A growth model is not a cultural model. A competitive model is not a motivation model. Using the right framework in the wrong domain feels exactly like using the wrong framework, which is part of why domain errors are so hard to catch from the inside.

The practical habit is to name the model out loud before you use it. "I am reasoning by analogy to how that market behaved last time" is a claim that can be examined. "The research says so" is not.

### Externalise the thinking

Most cognitive errors are invisible from the inside, which is why they survive. Writing and checklists work not because they make you smarter but because they turn a private, continuous process into an inspectable, finite artefact. Atul Gawande's argument in [The Checklist Manifesto](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/547201/the-checklist-manifesto-by-atul-gawande/) is that in complex work, the failures are almost never the ones where somebody did not know enough — they are ones where the right thing was known and simply did not get called at the right moment.

So: the reasoning goes on paper, in order, before the meeting rather than in the meeting. Predictions, probabilities and assumptions get written down with a date. The premortem reasons get written down by whoever thought of them, verbatim, so the good one survives the room.

None of this is clever, and that is the point. Quality of thinking is not improved by having more ideas; it is improved by installing a small number of procedures that catch the specific ways your own thinking fails, and then running them whether or not you feel like it. The advantage compounds quietly, over years, and it is mostly invisible to the people around you.

## The uncomfortable part: most of this is practice, not insight

Knowing the questions is not the same as asking them. The reason the outside view and premortems work is that they are scheduled, not remembered. When something is on the calendar, the person who would otherwise forget to look at base rates does look at them.

What I have adopted, and what is unremarkable but effective:

- **One page, three parts.** Diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions. Three headings, one page, written before the first action is scheduled. If it does not fit on a page, the thinking is not done.
- **A written record of the reasoning.** The bet, the odds, the key assumption, the date. Reviewed later regardless of the outcome, because the whole point is to grade the process rather than the result.
- **A premortem before anything expensive or hard to reverse.** It is the single highest-value fifteen minutes in the process and it is the one most often skipped.
- **A named review date**, with the decision-quality signals written down in advance, so the review is a test rather than a rationalisation exercise.
- **A deliberate hunt for the base rate**, and an honest "I could not find one" when there isn't one, which is itself information.

And one warning. Peter Drucker's line that [management is doing things right while leadership is doing the right things](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2021/04/09/doing-right/) is a permanent hum in the background when you work in delivery, where the measurable thing is always easier to talk about than the important thing. Worse, the moment a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure — [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law) — so strategic thinking decays into metric management unless someone keeps asking what the metric is a proxy for.

Strategy is a habit before it is a document. The document just makes the habit visible.
