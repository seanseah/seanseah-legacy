Met up with N for lunch. N was once part of my team, and is now serving notice before heading off for further studies. These catch-ups have become rarer as life gets busier, so I treasured this one. There is something different about lunch with someone you watched grow up professionally — the conversation skips the small talk and goes straight to the things that matter.

## Watching someone grow

When N first joined the team, there was raw potential, the kind you spot but cannot quite prove yet. You give such people work, guidance, and occasionally tough feedback, then you wait and hope the environment does its job.

Sitting across the table today, I could see how much has changed. As an individual, N is calmer and more grounded. More sure of self. The questions N asks are better questions now — less about how to do the task, more about why it matters and who it affects. That shift sounds small, but it is everything.

From the organisation's perspective, the growth is even more visible. N no longer thinks in terms of my task and my deliverable. N thinks in systems — how work flows between teams, how decisions land on stakeholders, how to make the whole machine run better rather than just one cog spin faster. People listen when N speaks. Not because of a title, but because N has earned the trust. That is the kind of authority you cannot appoint someone into.

## A leader for the future

I have worked with many competent people over the years. Competence is common. What is rare is the combination of competence and care — the willingness to carry others along while climbing. N has both. Watching N navigate discussions today, I caught myself thinking: here is a leader for the future. Not a future leader. A leader, for the future. The distinction matters. The capability already exists; the future is just waiting for N to arrive.

## The reminder I needed

Lunch also left me with a quiet reminder to myself.

Our jobs as leaders are not to remain indispensable. Our jobs are to pave the way for others — to transfer what we know, open doors we struggled to open, absorb the hard lessons early so they don't have to repeat them — until one day they are ready to take over from us. If nobody on the team can replace me, I have not been leading. I have been hoarding.

Succession is not an event at the end of a career. It is the daily work of every leader who takes the role seriously. The best compliment my team can ever pay me is to outgrow the team.

So this is my note to self, written down before the feeling fades: keep paving. Keep building people who will eventually sit in your chair and do it better. That is not a threat to your career. That is the proof it meant something.

Go well, N. Wherever the studies take you, the habits you built here travel with you — and somewhere down the road, there will be a team of your own that is better because you once sat in mine.
